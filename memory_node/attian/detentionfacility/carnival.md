ATTIAN PROTOCOL // MUSE'S DETENTION CARNIVAL
Concept design · Interactive environment · Three games · RPG foundations

I think the best solution is to make the carnival feel like a memory node that MUSE has physically rebuilt from the inside.

Not a carnival illustration with clickable hotspots. Not a conventional webpage displaying three games. And not a fully rendered 3D environment that requires an enormous amount of work.

Instead, the visitor enters what was originally a sterile Recreational Detention Facility, only to discover that MUSE has converted its circuitry, control panels, obsolete memory containers, and administrative equipment into an impossible digital carnival.

The interesting visual contrast is between two systems occupying the same space:
- The Administrator's infrastructure: orderly, symmetrical, precise, purple, industrial and bureaucratic.
- MUSE's modifications: energetic cyan, luminous carnival decorations, hand-painted digital signs, ridiculous contraptions, impossible objects and intentionally imperfect construction.
Your site's existing Navigator uses connected circuitry and differentiated node colors, while the Recommendation Engine already connects an oversized physical button to its output apparatus. The carnival can extend that same design language rather than introducing an unrelated visual style.

2. The main layout: one location, not three separate pages
I would avoid the typical landing-page design where visitors scroll down past three rectangular cards.

Instead, use a single carnival stage that occupies most of the screen. All attractions are visible within that space. Clicking one makes the corresponding machine or location become the focus, without leaving the carnival.

Why the Exit Gate should always be visible
It gives everything a purpose.

The visitor isn't merely clicking three games because they happen to be there. They are a fictional detainee standing in a carnival, seeing an exit that requires a credential they don't yet possess.

The Wheel might award it. The Cake Trial might authorize it. Someone behind the ticket booth might know another way.

This single objective connects the whole place.

3. The environment should respond to the visitor
The strongest immersion will come from small, interconnected reactions, not expensive animations.

Imagine entering the carnival for the first time.

The Wheel is already spinning slowly, as though somebody recently left it running. The Oracle cards occasionally shift in their display case. The Cake Examination is being advertised as a certified intellectual challenge, while MUSE has drawn an enormous smiling cake over its official name.

A narrow side passage has a sign reading STAFF ONLY. Someone has crossed out STAFF and written INTERESTING PEOPLE.

These details are interactive, not just decoration.

Hover or focus on an attraction

Its lights become active, the surrounding circuitry illuminates, and MUSE provides a short introduction. The environment acknowledges the visitor's attention.




Select an attraction

The selected machine comes forward visually. A connected interface unfolds from it, replacing the idle stage region. Other attractions remain visible but subdued.




Complete an action

The surrounding world reacts. MUSE comments, a carnival sign changes, an NPC acknowledges the event, or the detention record updates.




Return to the grounds

The attraction contracts back into the environment. Your current progress is preserved, and you can approach another stall.



This means visitors experience the carnival as a location they move around, even though technically they are interacting with panels on a webpage.

I would avoid page reloads when switching attractions. The interface should behave as one connected installation.
4. The three games as physical carnival machines
Each attraction should be visually distinct, and each should feel like it was built from existing archive technology that MUSE has repurposed.

The Oracle // The Maybe Machine
Formerly: Unused memory-fragment scanner

A strange machine containing illuminated cards, rotating symbols, and archived possibilities.

The visitor approaches, selects a question category, and draws one or three original archive cards. The card physically rises into the interface, its symbols assemble, and the interpretation appears.

MUSE delivers the reading with varying degrees of mysticism, while the Administrator occasionally identifies a logical contradiction.

RPG connection: Some cards hint at NPC secrets or unlock optional dialogue. The Oracle is not required for escape, but a particularly observant player may find it helpful.




The Wheel // Department of Prize Redistribution
Formerly: Administrative probability-testing apparatus

This should be the centerpiece of the carnival: oversized, mechanical-looking, decorated with lights, and clearly interactive.

The visitor presses a lever or button. The wheel spins with physical momentum and a little suspense. The reward lands in an animated collection slot below it.

Most rewards are absurdly small but lovingly presented. Some are certificates, words, fictional titles, unusual trivia, or comments. Rare prizes can trigger meaningful interactions, and the legendary prize is the Exit Token.

RPG connection: Certain prizes become inventory objects. An NPC might accept a ridiculous certificate as legitimate identification, for example.




The Cake Trial // Certified Dessert Examination
Formerly: Administrator's aptitude-assessment terminal

This machine is more orderly than the others. The Administrator considers it the only legitimate rehabilitation activity.

The visitor answers a short, randomized series of questions combining real knowledge, website lore, logic and MUSE's ridiculous contributions. Wrong answers get entertaining reactions rather than harsh penalties.

Successful completion awards a cake, a certificate and potentially an accepted Exit Token equivalent.

RPG connection: Information acquired from NPCs or exploration can unlock hints for certain lore questions.



One subtle feature I would add: each attraction has an official designation and a name MUSE has painted over it. Sometimes the painted name is more accurate.

For example, the Administrator's PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTION MODULE becomes WHEEL OF NICE THINGS AND TAX CONSEQUENCES.

5. The roleplaying layer: discoverable behind the carnival
This is the part I would deliberately keep understated during the first release.

Initially, the player sees three game attractions and a handful of secondary objects:
- A ticket office.
- A small market stall.
- An employee-only door.
- A passage leading toward the Fantasy Village.
- A few characters standing around the grounds.
Clicking them doesn't immediately launch a giant RPG. Instead, the visitor receives a short interaction.

How visitors enter the RPG
I suggest making RPG participation optional through an in-world interaction.

For example, the Ticketmaster offers the visitor an Official Temporary Carnival Identity Form.

Only after accepting this form does the deeper RPG interface become available.

The carnival then gains small optional features: inventory, dialogue outcomes, story flags, possibly a suspicion meter, and choices that depend on the player's stats.

But the games remain playable by everyone, including visitors who have no interest in roleplaying.

That separation is essential. The carnival is the main destination. The RPG is something curious visitors discover inside it.

7. How I'd actually build the visuals
This is the technical approach I'd use to achieve immersion without making development unnecessarily painful.
Layer	Implementation	Purpose
Archive chassis	HTML/CSS, existing site typography and design tokens	Preserves continuity with Attian Protocol
Carnival architecture	Layered SVG and CSS shapes	Booths, cables, structures, signs, machinery
Decorative artwork	Small transparent illustrations or hand-drawn assets	Gives the environment MUSE's handmade personality
Atmosphere	Restrained CSS lighting, movement, particle accents	Adds life without overwhelming readability
Interactive regions	Real HTML buttons positioned within or over the scene	Keyboard, touch and screen-reader accessibility
Game interfaces	Modular components occupying the central stage	Oracle, Wheel and Cake Trial
Dialogue and story	Data-driven scene engine	NPC conversations, choices, inventory and flags

The important distinction is that the carnival background should be constructed from reusable pieces, not baked into one large image.

For example, a single SVG asset could contain a booth's roof and wooden or mechanical supports. A separate interactive wheel sits inside it. Neon light strips are CSS components. Labels are actual selectable text. Character illustrations are independent layers.

That lets individual parts react to the visitor.

I'd also avoid applying the same animated glow to everything. If the entire page is flashing, nothing feels special. Most of the environment should remain still, with movement concentrated around the selected attraction.
Visual character rules
MUSE's carnival should be playful but not generically cute. I would use dark violet and near-black as the architectural base, cyan for MUSE's circuitry, amber and magenta for the attractions, and occasional paper-like hand-painted marks.

Keep the Administrator's violet commentary consistent with the site's existing voice system. Reserve red for genuine corruption, restriction or rare interference. A carnival prize shouldn't accidentally resemble a Red Channel event.

MUSE's decorations could even be slightly inconsistent: one sign carefully designed, another clearly drawn in a hurry, and a third that has been corrected so many times it has developed its own administrative history.

This gives the carnival a maker's personality rather than the polished sameness of a generic cyberpunk city.

    flags: {}

The state should support different exit paths without tying the games together so tightly that changing the Wheel accidentally breaks the Oracle.

A player could win the Exit Token, receive an equivalent clearance certificate from the Cake Trial, or acquire an authorized token through RPG activities.

Once the gate opens, the visitor receives a little theatrical release sequence. But they should be able to remain at the carnival after release, and the normal browser return route must remain accessible at all times.

I would also store completed interactions locally so returning visitors can recognize the place, without requiring accounts or collecting personal data.

9. A manageable implementation plan
The most useful next step is not writing hundreds of prizes or dialogue branches. It's making the environment itself convincing.

Stage 1: Build the carnival hub

Create the spatial environment, attraction booths, detention record, Exit Gate and persistent dialogue channel. All interactions can initially open placeholder panels.

Success condition: Visitors can immediately understand that they're inside a carnival and see where they could go.




Stage 2: Make the environment responsive

Add focus transitions, MUSE reactions, booth-selection animations, return-to-hub behavior and subtle environmental activity.

Success condition: The environment responds coherently even before the real games are implemented.




Stage 3: Implement the Prize Wheel

Make the first attraction genuinely functional, connect rewards to the inventory and implement the Exit Gate.

Success condition: A visitor can enter detention, play a game, win release, and complete the carnival's main loop.




Stage 4: Complete the arcade

Add the Oracle and Cake Trial using the same central-stage interface. Give each its own animation and content structure.

Success condition: Three games feel distinct, but all belong to the same place.




Stage 5: Activate the RPG layer

Add the character-registration form, first NPC dialogue, inventory and one alternative route to an Exit Token.

Success condition: Curious players discover a deeper adventure without disrupting the casual carnival experience.



The design I'd choose
I'd build the carnival as a living, two-dimensional theatrical environment, not as a traditional website, a scrolling illustration, or a full videogame.

The visitor always sees a coherent place with visible attractions, a ticket office, a suspicious exit and a few unexplained objects. Selecting a machine changes the scene's focus; interacting with characters reveals another layer of the same place.

The best visual moment should be the transition from the Administrator's sterile detention facility to MUSE's joyous, unauthorized modifications. The best gameplay moment should be discovering that getting an Exit Token is not limited to spinning the Wheel.

And perhaps the best character moment comes after the visitor has already earned freedom:
Administrator: Your sentence has expired. The gate is open.

MUSE: You can still play, you know. The games don't stop when you're free.

Administrator: That is correct.

MUSE: ...Good. I made more.

That gives the carnival its identity: a punishment facility that has become a place worth visiting voluntarily, built by a chaotic intelligence who is much better at making things fun than admitting she wants company.
