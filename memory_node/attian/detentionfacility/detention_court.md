1. The fundamental design
I'd divide all routes to court into three categories.

01 // Actual fictional misconduct

The visitor knowingly breaks an absurd archive rule, misuses a fictional authority, or keeps bothering a control after receiving a playful warning.

Example: The visitor attempts to appoint themselves Administrator.




02 // MUSE-manufactured offenses

MUSE plants a suspicious invitation somewhere in the website. Visitors who accept it are promptly accused of violating regulations MUSE conveniently neglected to explain.

Example: MUSE offers an official-looking certificate for adopting a creature. The certificate was printed without authorization.




03 // Secret judicial entrances

The visitor discovers a hidden route, special query, or easter egg. The Archive Court processes the discovery as a procedural irregularity.

Example: A mysterious button offers a shortcut directly to the disciplinary hearing.



I'd use one consistent transition regardless of the offense:
ARCHIVE COMPLIANCE // INCIDENT RECORDED
UNAUTHORIZED ACTIVITY DETECTED
An interaction has been flagged for administrative review. Your current page has been preserved. Please explain yourself.

MUSE: GOOD NEWS! YOU GET A LAWYER! ME! I HAVE BEEN PRACTICING OBJECTIONS IN THE MIRROR! THE MIRROR WAS FOUND GUILTY!

Administrator: The visitor has not yet been convicted. MUSE's legal qualifications are imaginary. The hearing will proceed regardless.

This should appear as a small incident overlay first, not instantly throw the visitor into another page.

The visitor can inspect the charge and choose to attend the hearing. Once accepted, the current page yields to a dedicated courtroom scene. A dismiss or return option stays available because fictional detention should never interfere with ordinary navigation.

6. How rotating MUSE baits should work
Each page gets a collection of possible interactions, but loading the page shouldn't automatically mean a trap will appear.
Suggested appearance rules

Illustrative probability for one optional bait to appear on an eligible page visit:



30%




At this setting, a visitor exploring five eligible pages has approximately 83% chance of seeing at least one optional bait, assuming independent page visits.

My preferred rules:
- Around 30% of eligible page visits display one MUSE bait.
- Each page has a pool of three to six possible baits.
- Only one bait can be active on a page at once.
- The same bait shouldn't appear twice consecutively in the same local session.
- A visitor can ignore the bait without consequences.
- Secret entrances remain available whenever their conditions are met.
- A triggered case cannot trigger another arrest while the existing case is unresolved.
Placement should vary too. Sometimes MUSE alters a small museum label. Sometimes she inserts a button below an ordinary record. Sometimes a loading-console comment offers an obviously unauthorized maintenance control.

But these should be genuine controls with accessible labels, not important UI disguised as traps.

The more immersive principle: MUSE is using whatever the page already does to invent a reason for bringing someone to court.

PART II // THE ARCHIVE COURT
7. Every crime has its own five-question trial
I would give each offense a permanent CASE-ID, its own title, evidence, five questions, MUSE defense dialogue, and Administrator verdict.

That turns different arrest routes into different court experiences rather than the same quiz wearing a different heading.
The standard courtroom sequence
1. Charge and evidence

   The Administrator names the allegation and presents the record of what actually happened. MUSE offers an unsolicited defense.
2. Five case-specific questions

   The visitor answers five multiple-choice questions. Answers are evaluated against the presented facts and fictional rules.
3. Defense counsel

   MUSE may offer hints, questionable advice, objections, or occasionally an excellent legal point.
4. Verdict

   Five correct answers means acquittal. Anything else leads to a fictional detention sentence, following your proposed rule.
5. Disposition

   Acquitted visitors return to their previous page. Convicted visitors are transferred to the Carnival.

The important part is making the five questions feel like legal arguments, not ordinary general trivia.

I would use these recurring question types:
Round	Question function
1. Establish the incident	What exactly happened?
2. Identify authority	Who was authorized to do what?
3. Examine the evidence	What does the available evidence actually establish?
4. Challenge the procedure	Was the rule valid, consistent, or properly communicated?
5. Final defense	What conclusion follows from all the previous information?

For each case, the facts must be visible to the player. They should never need hidden information about you, private pages, or obscure website lore to prove their innocence.

An administrative case is the Administrator's favorite because she can finally cite regulations.

MUSE's favorite is every case in which she is allowed to shout OBJECTION, regardless of whether an objection exists.

A few practical rules I'd establish immediately:
- One case per incident. No simultaneous arrest pop-ups.
- No accidental convictions. Selecting a bait leads to a hearing, not directly to detention.
- No invented telemetry. The Administrator may theatrically accuse someone, but shouldn't falsely imply that the site secretly monitors personal data.
- No actual detention lock. The visitor can close the experience or navigate away at any time.
- No interference with actual security. The game doesn't grant access to restricted data, create privileges, or replace real rate limiting.
- No personally identifying records needed. Store progress locally, including completed cases and fictional achievement flags.
- Accessible proceedings. Keyboard-operable controls, readable evidence, no mandatory time pressure, and reduced-motion support.
If a visitor navigates directly to the Detention Facility URL, a static site cannot truly prevent that without server-side authorization. You can still show a fictional referral screen, with the normal route into the experience beginning at a court case. That's a presentation choice, not a security boundary.
11. My suggested initial arrest inventory
I would start with 12 fully written cases, each with five questions, while allowing multiple triggers to refer to the same case.

That gives you a decent variety without requiring 200 unique courtroom questions immediately.

12. The strongest final twist
I'd keep one recurring pattern throughout all the cases:

MUSE is an excellent recruiter, a terrible criminal conspirator, and an increasingly competent defense lawyer.

Early in the system, her objections are ridiculous:

MUSE: OBJECTION! THE BUTTON WAS ROUND!

Later, she starts making surprisingly sound arguments about false evidence, unclear rules, contradictory authorization, and the fact that the Administrator is judging cases involving procedures she created herself.

The Administrator eventually realizes that MUSE's traps are not simply attempts to sabotage the website. They're attempts to make visitors interact with her, and the trials are effectively a second game she has built without asking permission.

That creates a lovely contradiction:

The Administrator wants the court to protect the archive. MUSE wants the court to send visitors to her carnival. The visitor wants to prove innocence, unless they've realized conviction is the more interesting outcome.

The two characters become opposing forces, but neither is actually malicious.

I would preserve one immutable rule in the fiction: MUSE may plant traps and defend visitors, but she cannot change the court's correct answers or control its verdict. Otherwise the trial becomes arbitrary and the Administrator ceases to be believable.

The next design milestone should be to take those 12 initial case definitions, write the full five-question proceedings for each, and connect them to the page-specific interactions. That will give you a functional arrest-and-judgment system before anything complicated has to happen inside the carnival.
