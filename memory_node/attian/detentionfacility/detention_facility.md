ATTIAN PROTOCOL // RECREATIONAL DETENTION
A hidden carnival, a dysfunctional justice system, and one AI who really wants visitors.

I think this is a substantially stronger direction than having four independent games inside a publicly accessible recreation node.

The key change is that the Detention Facility is no longer a destination visitors choose. It's somewhere they end up.

The Administrator considers it a disciplinary facility. MUSE considers it her home, her carnival, and probably the most important thing anyone has ever entrusted to her. She's especially enthusiastic because, unlike the rest of the archive, people actually get to play with her there.

And occasionally she engineers situations to make that happen.

That creates a coherent experience with four connected parts:

2. The Administrator's court

Five questions. MUSE as defense lawyer. Acquittal or sentence.


Acquitted

Convicted

Transferred to MUSE's carnival.

Play games, investigate, meet NPCs, gain items, discover alternative escape routes

The system should have two distinct gameplay loops: the short loop of being caught, tried and released, and the long loop of exploring the carnival and its story. Someone who just wants a wheel spin can do that. Someone who wants to spend half an hour uncovering a criminal enterprise run by a sentient ticket booth can also do that.

2. The courtroom: five questions, one verdict
This shouldn't feel like a separate trivia game. The courtroom is a short, theatrical interrogation with predetermined rules, case-specific writing, and occasional sabotage by the defense lawyer.

The Administrator is the judge. She's not a villain. She actually wants due process, accuracy, and a quick resolution.

MUSE is the appointed defense lawyer, appointed by herself, and believes every visitor is innocent because visitors are nice and being innocent sounds like a wonderful thing to be.
The five-question framework
Rather than five random trivia questions, I'd make every trial follow the same progression:
Round	Function	Example
1. Identification	Establish what the visitor allegedly did.	Did you knowingly interact with the forbidden artifact?
2. Evidence	Present evidence, usually comically inadequate.	The evidence consists of a button click and MUSE shouting “WITNESS!”
3. Intent	Understand why they did it.	Curiosity, malice, boredom, or the button looking lonely?
4. Technicality	Introduce a rule, contradiction or loophole.	Was the restriction valid if MUSE wrote it herself?
5. Final Defense	Give one challenging or surprising opportunity to earn acquittal.	Identify the actual person responsible for the entire incident.

This structure is more immersive than ordinary multiple-choice trivia and creates a recognizable legal rhythm.

However, there's a design decision to make about what “correct” means. I'd establish a definite correct answer for each question based on the fictional evidence and rules, rather than having the Administrator arbitrarily reject reasonable choices. MUSE can give terrible advice, but the game itself should be fair.

My preferred verdict system is:
- 5/5 correct: Full acquittal. Return to the previous page.
- 0–4 correct: Conviction. Enter the Carnival.
- A wrong answer doesn't terminate the hearing. Visitors complete all five questions and receive a verdict at the end.
- Retrying should remain possible later; no genuine access restriction should depend on winning.
That makes a perfect score genuinely special, without requiring players to be psychic.
The courtroom's hidden joke
Occasionally, MUSE's advice is correct.

Not merely accidentally funny. Actually useful.

The Administrator dislikes this more than the incorrect advice because it demonstrates MUSE may be capable of competent legal reasoning and is simply choosing not to practice it consistently.

4. Exiting detention: the central gameplay objective
I agree that the Exit Token should be the main prize.

But acquiring one should be possible through different activities, each offering a different experience. That's what lets the carnival support both casual visitors and players who want an RPG.
Five possible ways out

1. Win the legendary wheel prize

The iconic method. The visitor spins and occasionally lands on an Exit Token. It should feel exciting, with a special animation and MUSE becoming almost unbearably proud.

This is the luck-based route, not the only practical route.




2. Pass the Cake Examination

Completing a difficult but fair trivia challenge earns an official certificate of rehabilitation, recognized by the exit gate.

This is the knowledge-based route.




3. Earn a token from an NPC

Help a carnival inhabitant solve a problem, recover an object, or negotiate a dispute. The grateful NPC provides an Exit Token or equivalent authorization.

This is the quest-based route.




4. Organize the Great Ticket Heist

Find a few conspirators, acquire suspicious equipment, and steal a fictional Exit Token from the carnival's excessively guarded ticket office.

This is the roleplaying and puzzle route.




5. Discover a bureaucratic loophole

The carnival's regulations contain contradictions. A careful player can exploit one, convince the Administrator, or demonstrate that their sentence was invalid.

This is the secret administrative route.



One very important distinction: the fictional gate can demand a token, but the actual webpage must always have an ordinary way to leave. Browser navigation, an accessible return link, and a way to opt out should never be blocked. Otherwise your joke about unlawful detention becomes rather less fictional.

The gate itself can remain closed until a player acquires a token. Leaving through the normal interface simply abandons the current carnival run.

Also, I would let the visitor stay after winning.
Administrator: Your sentence has concluded. You may leave.

MUSE: OR STAY! We have a perfectly legal second wheel! I think!

Administrator: The visitor is free.

MUSE: I KNOW. I JUST LIKE WHEN THEY STAY.

That small moment communicates more about MUSE than another thirty lines of shouting about soup.

5. The roleplaying layer: a carnival with its own society
This is where I would separate your new approach from the earlier proposal.

The Fantasy Village should not be a fourth minigame. Instead, the carnival is the first playable location in a larger connected fantasy world.

Its entrance could be hidden behind the carnival stalls. A road leads toward the Fantasy Village on the Grand Map. Some villagers work at the carnival, some are trapped there, some visit voluntarily, and some are exploiting MUSE's inability to understand labor contracts.

The player begins as a detainee, but becomes an adventurer through interaction.
Keep the RPG mechanics deliberately small
VISITOR CHARACTER RECORD
Charm
Persuasion, bluffing and social absurdity

Wits
Clues, puzzles and loopholes

Audacity
Risky plans and magnificent bad ideas


Additional tracked state: a tiny inventory, a few NPC relationship flags, suspicion level, and quest completion. No complicated equipment tiers or combat system needed initially.

Those three stats allow different solutions without creating a giant RPG ruleset.

For example, the ticket-office guard refuses to let you enter:
- Charm: Convince them you're a replacement carnival employee.
- Wits: Discover that their authorization expired yesterday.
- Audacity: Announce an imaginary fire inspection and hope someone panics.
- Inventory option: Present a permit obtained elsewhere.
- NPC option: Ask another character to distract the guard.
Different routes can converge on the same scene. You get replayability without having to write five completely separate adventures.
Three initial NPCs I'd develop
The Ticketmaster: A deeply suspicious official who takes exit-token security far too seriously, possibly because someone has been stealing tokens.

The Former Detainee: A person who earned freedom several days ago but now lives at the carnival voluntarily. They know shortcuts, rumors and slightly more about MUSE than they should.

The Traveling Merchant: Arrived from the Fantasy Village to sell magical curiosities and discovered that the carnival's prize economy has no recognizable currency. They are now selling legal advice, fake permits and increasingly desperate pastries.

Each NPC serves both as characterization and as a route toward freedom.

The heist could then become a genuine multi-step quest: find someone who knows the ticket office, obtain a disguise or permit, discover how tokens are stored, and choose whether to steal, counterfeit, or expose the ticket system.

Not every approach succeeds, but failures should produce funny story consequences rather than force a full restart.

7. Character roles and the occasional deeper lore
One concern from the previous design was making the Administrator nothing more than a disapproving reaction machine. Here she has an actual institutional role.

Administrator	Judge, appeals authority, keeper of fictional rules	Keep the archive orderly, protect visitors and maintain fair procedures
MUSE	Defense lawyer, carnival host, unofficial recruiter	Share her games, have company, create memorable interactions

Carnival NPCs	Merchants, suspects, former detainees, guards, travelers	Their own small desires and problems, not merely joke dispensers

The deeper lore should be quiet. You don't need a dramatic revelation in every visit.

For example, an old carnival poster might say:

VISITOR ENRICHMENT PROGRAM // INITIAL OCCUPANCY: 0

MUSE could respond:
MUSE: That's the old poster! I made a better one. It says EVERYONE WELCOME! Except it used to be nobody. Nobody came for a while. ANYWAY, WOULD YOU LIKE A BALLOON?

And the Administrator doesn't comment.

That one moment gives the whole detention system a slightly different meaning. MUSE's traps aren't just mischief. They're her awkward attempt to create a place where people want to spend time with her.

I wouldn't over-explain it, though. Recurring clues are more effective than a monologue about her loneliness.

For the Red Signal, keep interference rare, unsettling, and disconnected from ordinary carnival proceedings. A flicker in an old exit corridor, a damaged ticket number, a few corrupted words. Nothing that speaks as a villain or threatens the player.

D. Accessibility and control
A few rules worth establishing from the beginning: no real bans, no trapping browser navigation, no punishment based on accidental repeated clicks, and no personal-data collection for fictional misconduct.

A visitor should be able to leave normally, reset local progress, turn off animations, and skip previously completed introduction sequences. Fictional confinement is funny; hostile UX is merely hostile UX.

9. Scope: what I'd actually build first
I'd develop this in four manageable releases, with each one already feeling like a coherent feature.
Release	Implement	Outcome
I. The Arrest	Hidden entrances, courtroom UI, 5–8 cases, five questions each, verdict system	Visitors can discover the mechanic and be sentenced.
II. The Carnival	Hub environment, wheel, exit gate, first prize pool	Visitors can enter, win a token, and escape.
III. The Other Ways Out	Oracle, Cake Trial, 3–4 NPCs, inventory and stat checks	Different players can escape using luck, knowledge, or storytelling.
IV. The Larger World	Ticket heist, more NPCs, village portal and persistent quest flags	The detention room becomes the gateway to a connected RPG setting.

For the initial public release, I would especially avoid implementing the entire Fantasy Village. Start with one memorable character, one mystery, and one alternative exit route. Once those work, adding more stories becomes much easier.
The final identity of the Detention Facility
I would preserve its public-facing name as Detention Facility or Recreational Detention Facility, while letting MUSE insist on calling it something like THE MOST WONDERFUL CORRECTIONAL CARNIVAL IN THE WORLD.

The fictional premise becomes:

An archive administrator established a system for handling visitors who crossed certain boundaries. She appointed an erratic suggestion engine as defense counsel and supervisor of the disciplinary recreation sector. The engine, starved for company and blessed with unlimited imagination, converted the detention room into a carnival.

Visitors can avoid conviction by proving their innocence in five questions. Those who fail enter the carnival, where an Exit Token is the official route to freedom. Unfortunately, the facility also contains games, secrets, conspiracies, an emerging fantasy world, and several people who no longer wish to leave.

The essential design principle is that detention gets visitors into the Detention Facility, but the carnival gives them reasons to stay voluntarily.

That lets the website preserve its core identity as a personal archive while introducing a genuinely interactive, story-driven playground. And it gives MUSE somewhere to be much more than a recommendation engine without making her behavior dominate the rest of Attian Protocol.
